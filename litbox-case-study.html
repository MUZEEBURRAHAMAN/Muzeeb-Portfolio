01What we're building

Building a unified legal file layer inside the ecosystem

LitBox is a dedicated, matter-aware file management platform built to power the entire Omnis AI legal suite. We replaced fragmented third-party drive dependencies and unaudited sharing links with a single owned foundation.

Here is how LitBox tackles three foundational bottlenecks across the legal document lifecycle.

Third-party drive detours before every legal workflow

The problem
What we're building
A file had to exist in Dropbox, Box, or Google Drive before it could touch any Omnis AI product. Attorneys faced repetitive re-authorizations, disconnected accounts, and fragmented storage silos with zero unified matter context.
A unified legal file workspace where documents live natively alongside active matters. LitBox removes vendor detours with an owned, matter-organized file directory every tool in the firm can reach.

External file sharing without legal privilege or chain of custody

The problem
What we're building
Requesting confidential records via external drive links exposed sensitive litigation data to unverified vendor security models. Firms lacked automated folder routing, expiration controls, pin security, and matter-level audit logging.
Matter-governed File Requests. Clients upload sensitive files directly into matter folders through pin-protected links with custom expirations and real-time audit trails.

Disconnected product silos requiring manual file transfers

The problem
What we're building
CasePro, OmnisProof, and MedChron each handled file ingestion in isolation. Attorneys repeatedly downloaded files and re-uploaded them into separate tools, creating duplicate copies and version-control chaos.
A shared ecosystem file layer that integrates directly into every Omnis AI product. Through embedded pickers and native APIs, products attach evidence, sign agreements, and extract chronologies without leaving their workflow.
The goal

Turn fragmented third-party file storage into a unified, secure document foundation that powers every product and collaboration workflow across the legal ecosystem.

02Why this, why first · Sequencing the roadmap

Why storage had to ship before the rest of the roadmap

Omnis AI had a full slate of legal products planned (CasePro, OmnisProof, MedChron, NotaryNow, LitDraft and more), each on its own launch timeline. But every one of them touches a file at some point: CasePro attaches evidence to a matter, OmnisProof signs a document, MedChron organizes medical records chronologically, NotaryNow notarizes a document. If each product solved "where do files live" on its own, the platform would end up with as many storage implementations, security models, and third-party integrations as it had products, instead of one.

That's why LitBox needed to exist before most of those products launched, not after. Shipping it first meant everything built afterward could assume a shared, owned file layer from day one, instead of retrofitting one once four or five products already had their own quiet workarounds.

CasePro

Evidence and case files need to be tied to the matter record it manages. LitBox is where that evidence actually lives.

OmnisProof

E-signature needs a document to sign, in place, without a round trip through a downloaded file.

MedChron

Medical chronologies are built from records that need a single, permission-aware source instead of scattered copies.

NotaryNow

Notarization needs the same document trust model as e-signature: one owned layer, not a fourth vendor integration.

My role

I led this end to end: framing the three problems above from patterns across the products I'd already worked on, the research that grounded the case for owning storage instead of integrating another vendor, the information architecture and permission model, the full UI system, and the integration points, like the "Import from LitBox" pattern used inside other Omnis AI products, that make LitBox a layer the rest of the ecosystem actually plugs into, not a fourth silo next to the other three.

The opening insightLitBox didn't start from a single support ticket or a usability session I ran. It started from a pattern across the other Omnis AI products I'd already worked on: AutoDoc needed a document to process, OmnisProof needed a document to sign, CasePro needed a document attached to a matter, and each one was quietly solving "where does the file live" by pointing a lawyer at a third-party drive. The gap wasn't "lawyers need a nicer Dropbox." It was that the ecosystem had no first-party file layer of its own.

03Research & security context · Why it matters

Why secure storage is a different problem for a law firm

A generic drive protects files. A law firm's drive has to protect privilege: client identities, litigation strategy, medical and financial records, and communications that are confidential by professional obligation, not just by preference. That distinction is what "secure storage" has to mean for LitBox, and it's worth grounding in real incidents rather than abstract risk language.

Three research methods, combined

No single method could carry the case for owning a storage layer on its own. Secondary research proves the risk is real, a competitive teardown proves the gap is real, and internal conversations proved the demand across products was real. All three live as source material inside the project's Figma file, not just as claims in this case study.

Why I'm not using these as marketingAll five incidents targeted large, well-resourced firms with existing security programs. None of them prove LitBox would have prevented anything, and I'm not implying otherwise. What they do establish: social engineering, not exotic malware, was the entry point in at least three of the five, and every firm's own IT/security staff were the impersonation target. That's a specific, citable pattern: "harden the human workflow around file requests," not "buy more encryption."

The rule lawyers actually have to follow

Lawyers aren't just choosing security software out of caution; a real ethics rule requires it. ABA Model Rule 1.6(c) says a lawyer must make "reasonable efforts" to stop a client's information from leaking, on purpose or by accident. A companion rule says a lawyer has to understand the tools they're using well enough to know the risk. Neither rule names a specific product or certification, just "reasonable" and "informed." That's a lower bar than "encrypted" or "fully compliant," which is exactly why I'm not putting either word on LitBox without proof behind it.

What "secure" actually has to include

Legal-software vendors (Clio, Sync) and incident write-ups from CloudLex converge on the same short list. This describes their own practices, not independent research, but the list is a useful checklist against a consumer drive:

  • Encryption, while a file moves and while it sits in storage.
  • Multi-factor login: a password alone isn't enough to get in.
  • Per-person, per-file roles, not one link anyone can forward.
  • A record of who did what, not just that a file was sent somewhere.

Sync's own material is blunt about the gap: consumer tools are "designed for a broad audience" and usually skip the access controls and audit trails a firm specifically needs.

What LitBox does not claimLitBox does not currently claim SOC 2 certification, HIPAA compliance, zero-knowledge or end-to-end encryption, or attorney-client-privilege protection as shipped, verified capabilities. Encryption in transit and at rest, role-based permissions, and broader audit-export tooling are design targets reflected in the product's own module-architecture research. Treat anything above not tied to a screenshot elsewhere in this case study as a proposed capability requiring technical and business validation, not a shipped guarantee. The two security-adjacent surfaces actually built and shown here are per-file role sharing and the Audit Timeline, both in Section 10.

04Competitive landscape · Why not just use Drive or Dropbox

Established storage products aren't wrong. They're not built for a legal matter

Google Drive, Dropbox, and Box are mature, well-engineered products used by millions of people, including plenty of law firms. The honest question isn't "are they secure." It's "do they model the thing a law firm actually organizes around: a matter, not a folder."

General purpose

Google Drive / Dropbox

  • Reliable, familiar, deep integration with everyday office tools.
  • Sharing is file- or folder-scoped, not matter-scoped: a link can outlive the reason it was created.
  • No native concept of a "case," a retention policy, or a legal hold.
  • Search finds a file by name or content, not by which matter it belongs to.
Enterprise governance

Box

  • Strongest of the general-purpose tools on admin controls, retention, and compliance reporting.
  • Still organizes around folders and enterprise groups, not legal matters or case teams.
  • No native link into document processing, e-signature, or case-management workflows built for law firms.
Privacy-first

Sync.com

  • Markets end-to-end, zero-knowledge encryption as a core differentiator for sensitive files.
  • Still a standalone drive: no matter context, no case-team permission model, no ecosystem it plugs into.
Legal-first

LitBox

  • Files and folders behave the way Drive and Dropbox users already expect: same learning curve, familiar patterns.
  • Adds a matter-aware permission model, an audit timeline, and File Requests built for external submitters.
  • Connects natively into AutoDoc and OmnisProof today, with CasePro and the rest of the ecosystem as the roadmap.

Competitive SWOT

The same three competitors, scored honestly against LitBox rather than dismissed outright, including where LitBox itself is genuinely weaker today.

See the full SWOT for Drive, Dropbox, Box and Sync.com

Dropbox / Google Drive

  • Strengths: familiar to nearly every user, deep Office and Workspace integration, mature mobile and desktop sync.
  • Weaknesses: no matter context, folder- or file-scoped sharing only, no legal-specific audit trail.
  • Opportunities: a firm frustrated by re-uploading the same file into three tools is a switching moment.
  • Threats: both could add "legal mode" features faster than a startup can build ecosystem integrations.

Box

  • Strengths: the strongest admin, governance and retention tooling of the three general-purpose tools.
  • Weaknesses: organized around departments and folders, not matters; no native e-signature or document-intelligence handoff.
  • Opportunities: its governance strength proves firms will pay for compliance features; LitBox's Audit Timeline targets the same instinct.
  • Threats: Box already sells into the enterprise-legal segment LitBox needs; incumbency is a real barrier.

Sync.com

  • Strengths: zero-knowledge encryption marketed directly at privacy-conscious buyers, including law firms.
  • Weaknesses: still a standalone drive, with no matter model, no case-team permissions, nothing to plug into.
  • Opportunities: proves firms will pay a premium for "more secure than Dropbox," which LitBox can pair with ecosystem integration.
  • Threats: the closest thing to a shortcut: a firm could get "secure enough" from Sync without ever needing LitBox, exactly the case made in Section 01's third problem.
Where LitBox is still behindDrive, Dropbox, and Box have years of reliability data, mobile and desktop sync clients, and integrations LitBox doesn't have yet, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and a mature third-party app ecosystem among them. A firm already deep in one of those tools has real switching costs LitBox has to justify, not assume away.

05Solutions considered · Options on the table

Three places the shared file layer could have lived. Two were faster to ship, and wrong

Once "every product needs files and none of them own storage" was clear (Section 02), the fix wasn't obvious yet; it rarely is. These are the three real architectural paths I weighed, not a polished single recommendation dressed up after the fact. The fuller research behind each option (module notes, competitor teardown, stakeholder input) lives as its own set of pages inside the project's Figma file.

Rejected

A · Each product keeps its own storage

  • LitBox becomes a thin UI opened from inside AutoDoc or CasePro: no shared backend, just a nicer picker.
  • Fastest to ship, since every product already has some upload mechanism to build on.

Doesn't fix fragmentation, it just re-skins it. Every product still holds its own copy of a file, with no shared audit trail connecting them.

Rejected

B · Fold storage into CasePro

  • Build file management as a module inside the case-management CRM, since matters already live there.
  • The "obvious" answer: CasePro already owns the concept of a matter.

Ties every other product's file access to CasePro's release cycle and permission model. MedChron and LitDraft would still need their own workarounds to reach a file.

Chosen

C · A standalone layer, embeddable everywhere

  • LitBox owns a file once, and exposes a search and picker surface any product can call into, not just link out to.
  • Slower to ship than A, more independent than B.

The only option where AutoDoc, OmnisProof, MedChron, and LitDraft can each connect on their own timeline, without depending on each other or on CasePro shipping first.

The part I didn't expect to prove outOption C was a bet that other product teams would actually build against LitBox rather than route around it. Section 15's real staging screenshots, with LitBox's search running live inside MedChron and AutoDoc, are the closest thing this case study has to that bet paying off.

06Product strategy · Defining the product

Turning research into a defensible scope, not a feature wishlist

Problem statementLaw firms working inside the Omnis AI ecosystem have no shared, secure place to store case files, so every product either builds its own storage or depends on a general-purpose drive that has no concept of a legal matter, a case team, or an audit trail.
Opportunity statementA file layer purpose-built for legal matters, with familiar Drive-like interaction patterns, can become the shared foundation the rest of the ecosystem reads from and writes to, instead of each product solving storage on its own.

Product principles

Familiar first, legal second

The core file-management surface should feel like Drive or Dropbox on first use. The legal-specific value layers on top, without forcing a new mental model for basic file handling.

Every action leaves a record

Upload, download, share, permission change, restore, delete: the Audit Timeline exists so nothing sensitive happens invisibly.

A platform, not a silo

A file uploaded to LitBox should be reachable from the products that need it, starting with AutoDoc and OmnisProof, instead of requiring a re-upload for every downstream tool.

Who it's for

Not every persona needs the same surface. That shaped the IA more than any single feature request did.

AttorneysNeed fast retrieval and confidence that a shared file is the current version.
Paralegals and litigation supportThe heaviest daily users: uploading, organizing, and requesting files from clients and co-counsel.
Firm admins and ITOwn storage oversight, user provisioning, and audit exports. A distinct admin surface (Storage Management, Audit Logs & Activity) exists specifically for this group.

MVP scope

The internal-beta scope covers the core file-management loop end to end (upload, organize, preview, share, request, restore and delete, search, and audit) plus the confirmed ecosystem connections: AutoDoc via File Requests, OmnisProof via in-document e-signature, and embedded search live inside MedChron and AutoDoc itself (Section 15). A conversational AI assistant, deeper CasePro matter-linking, and broader third-party integrations are explicitly out of MVP scope; Section 13 shows what that AI question actually looks like.

The Audit Timeline, the one security surface actually built

Every upload, download, share, rename, permission change, and restore is written to a single timeline, filterable by action type, with the file, the user and role, and a timestamp on every row. Alongside the roles above, this is the concrete answer to "how would a firm know who touched a file": not a proposed capability, something actually built.

Admin gets a different dashboard, not a hidden toggle

Storage Management is a separate surface for firm admins (total usage, storage by file type, per-user breakdowns, and quota alerts) rather than a setting buried inside the same view every attorney and paralegal sees. Keeping it separate meant the day-to-day workspace never had to compromise its simplicity to accommodate governance features most users will never open.

Real iteration, not just a first draftA dedicated UI-testing pass on these screens produced direct, unglamorous feedback I then designed against: tightening card padding, minimum card widths so a grid doesn't collapse awkwardly on narrower viewports, tooltips for truncated file and folder names instead of letting them clip silently, and standardizing sidebar and header components against the shared component library rather than letting each screen drift. None of that shows up in a polished screenshot, but it's the difference between a mockup and a system someone else can extend.

11Five features, in detail · The internal beta

Five surfaces that carry most of the daily weight

Everything below is a real screen from the shipped Figma file, not a mockup built for this case study.

Dashboard, with full document detail

The Dashboard is data-dense on purpose: storage usage, file and folder counts, and recent activity at a glance, so a firm admin isn't hunting for that information in a separate settings screen. Opening any file surfaces a detail drawer with two tabs: Document Details (who has access, folder metadata, description) and Document Activity (a timeline of edits, renames, and inline comments from the team).

Sharing, with per-person roles

Rather than one public link everyone gets the same access to, sharing a file assigns each person a role (Owner, Editor, or Viewer) by email, without overclaiming a password and expiry system that isn't in the shipped file yet. The reference alongside it shows what a fuller privacy and password layer looks like in a mature product, and the direction the sharing model is headed.

Storage Management, admin only

A separate permission tier, not a hidden toggle in the same screen everyone sees. Firm admins get organization-wide storage totals, a breakdown by file type, and a per-user table with quota usage: the same screen that would back the "Export Report" a firm's IT lead needs for a compliance review.

Tag management, Finder-style

Colored tags that work across file types (a document, an image, and a video can all carry the same tag), closer to macOS Finder tags than to a folder-only scheme. A file can carry more than one tag, so "matter" and "document type" don't have to compete for the same folder slot.

Global search

Search scoped to All Modules, Files/Documents, Folders, or Recent Files, with a clear empty state rather than a blank screen when a query comes back with nothing. Section 12 covers what a populated result looks like (path, page, and highlighted snippet) once AutoDoc has extracted a file's text.

The AI assistant question

The module-architecture research behind LitBox sketches use cases like "find all medical records related to this matter" or "summarize these documents": a conversational layer on top of content search. Any answer would need a source citation back to the exact file and page, retrieval would need to respect the same permission model as manual search, and it would sit on top of the existing search index rather than replace it.

One assistant, sitting on top of a search index it doesn't have to rebuild

16Security & trust · Why it matters for a law firm

Every pattern in this section exists because privilege, not preference, is on the line

Section 03 made the case with documented breaches and an ethics rule; this is the shorter, practical version: the six trust surfaces that actually show up in the product, gathered in one place.

Permission-based access

Every file's visibility is scoped to named people and their role, not "anyone with the org login," the same per-person model as Section 11's sharing feature.

Secure file sharing

Owner, Editor, or Viewer, assigned per person, per file, with no default public-link option to reach for when a scoped share takes one extra click.

Clear access visibility

The Document Details drawer's "Who has access" list (Section 11) lets a paralegal check who can see a file without opening a separate admin screen.

Audit logs & activity

Upload, download, share, permission change, restore, delete: every action lands in the Audit Timeline, the one surface the product principles (Section 06) single out by name.

Secure file requests

External submitters never get a LitBox account. A request carries a deadline, an optional password, and a defined destination folder instead of an open-ended inbox.

AI permission awareness

Section 13's concept screens mark every AI result "You have access" or "Restricted" before anything else: a design requirement, not a feature the assistant gets to skip.

18Final experience & impact · Where this stands

What I can claim, what I can't yet, and what I'd do differently

Zoom out from any single screen and the shift is structural: Omnis AI went from a set of products that each quietly solved "where do files live" on its own, to a platform with one file layer the rest of it reads from and writes to. AutoDoc picks up files without a manual re-upload. OmnisProof signs a document in place. MedChron and LitDraft search the same index LitBox itself searches. None of that required a firm to change how they work; it required Omnis AI to stop asking every product to solve storage twice.

For the firm

  • One place to upload a file instead of one per tool it needs to reach.
  • A permission model and audit trail built for a legal matter, not adapted from a generic drive.
  • Fewer uncontrolled public links, because a scoped File Request or a per-person role is the default path, not an extra step.

For the ecosystem

  • New products (NotaryNow, deeper CasePro matter-linking) can assume a shared file layer exists instead of building their own.
  • Search and AI-assisted workflows (Section 13) get more useful the more products connect to the same index, not less.
  • A file's security posture no longer depends on which third-party vendor happened to hold it that week.

None of this makes LitBox "done." It makes the third problem from Section 01, an ecosystem that wasn't actually one, no longer true by default. That's the specific claim this case study is willing to make.

Impact, honestly framed

Measured

None yet. LitBox is in internal beta with no usage baseline to report. I'm not going to invent adoption numbers or time-savings percentages to make this section look stronger.

Observed

AutoDoc and OmnisProof integrations work end to end in the prototype, and staging screenshots confirm search is already live inside MedChron and AutoDoc. A structured UI-QA pass produced concrete, actionable fixes I designed against: real iteration, even without formal usability-test data.

Expected

Reduced re-uploading across AutoDoc and OmnisProof workflows, a defensible audit trail for firms handling sensitive files, and fewer uncontrolled public links: all hypotheses to validate once real usage exists, not claims to make today.

My role

I owned LitBox's product design end to end: the competitive and module-architecture research that scoped what "parity with Dropbox and Box" actually required, the information architecture, the full UI system across file management, sharing, search, audit, and admin, and the design of its live ecosystem connections into AutoDoc, OmnisProof, MedChron, and LitDraft. The empty-state, error-state, and permission-state documentation (more than two dozen scenarios specified before a single one shipped) was mine to define, not a gap engineering had to fill in later.

What I learned

Shared infrastructure is a different job

LitBox's users aren't just attorneys and paralegals; they're also AutoDoc and OmnisProof. A broken assumption in a file's metadata breaks someone else's product, not just this one.

A parity checklist isn't a strategy

The Dropbox and Box feature-parity research was necessary groundwork, but the real differentiation came from two questions a parity table can't answer: which matter does this belong to, and who downstream needs it.

Security language has to earn its claims

It was easier to write "SOC 2 compliant" into an early feature list than to confirm it was true. Separating planned from verified capability was a discipline I had to actively maintain, not a default.

Edge cases first is what makes it feel real

Designing 25+ empty and error states before the happy path ships is the difference between a prototype and a demo that only works when everything goes right.

LitBox's real test isn't whether its file grid looks as good as Dropbox's. It's whether AutoDoc and OmnisProof keep needing fewer workarounds the more of it ships. That's the metric an infrastructure product actually has to answer to.
