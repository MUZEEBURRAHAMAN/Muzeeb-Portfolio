CASE STUDY Product Designer · Legal Tech SaaS · Secure File Management

Every product in the ecosystem depended on Dropbox, Box, or Google Drive to hold a client's files. So I designed LitBox.

Before LitBox, a lawyer uploaded to a third-party drive first, then connected that account to whichever Omnis AI product needed the file — one integration per tool, per product, on a vendor Omnis AI didn't own and couldn't fully vouch for. LitBox replaces that dependency with a secure, matter-aware storage layer built inside the ecosystem itself.

01 / THE PROBLEM
Before LitBox

Omnis AI called itself an ecosystem. Its files said otherwise.

Before LitBox, a document's journey never started inside Omnis AI. A lawyer had a file, and the first move was never "open CasePro" — it was "open Dropbox, or Box, or Google Drive." Only after the file existed in one of those third-party tools could it be connected, one integration at a time, to whichever Omnis AI product actually needed it.

That habit — third party first, Omnis AI second — created three compounding problems, and it's the reason LitBox exists.

1. A Third-Party Detour Before Every Product

  • A file had to exist in Dropbox, Box, or Google Drive before it could touch anything Omnis AI built.
  • Then it had to be connected or re-authorized separately inside CasePro, inside OmnisProof, inside MedChron — one integration per tool, per product.
  • The same document could need three separate third-party connections just to be usable across a platform that was supposed to be one system.

2. Security We Couldn't Fully Vouch For

  • The moment a client's file left Omnis AI and landed in someone else's storage, we inherited that vendor's security posture.
  • We couldn't tell a firm "your data is 100% secure" while it was sitting in software we didn't build and couldn't audit — and the legal industry has no shortage of breach headlines proving that risk is real.

3. An Ecosystem That Wasn't Actually One

  • Omnis AI's pitch is that CasePro, OmnisProof, MedChron, LitDraft, and the rest work as one connected platform.
  • If the layer holding every product's files is a rented vendor account, that claim only holds at the marketing level.
  • Even a more secure third-party option — something like Sync.com — would still leave storage as a dependency instead of a layer the rest of the platform is actually built on. The fix wasn't a better vendor. It was owning the layer outright.
The Opening Insight LitBox didn't start from a single support ticket or a usability session I ran. It started from a pattern across the other Omnis AI products I'd already worked on: AutoDoc needed a document to process, OmnisProof needed a document to sign, CasePro needed a document attached to a matter — and each one was quietly solving "where does the file live" by pointing a lawyer at a third-party drive. The gap wasn't "lawyers need a nicer Dropbox." It was that the ecosystem had no first-party file layer of its own.

02 / WHY THIS, WHY FIRST
Sequencing The Roadmap

Why storage had to ship before the rest of the roadmap

Omnis AI had a full slate of legal products planned — CasePro, OmnisProof, MedChron, NotaryNow, LitDraft, and more — each on its own launch timeline. But every one of them touches a file at some point: CasePro attaches evidence to a matter, OmnisProof signs a document, MedChron organizes medical records chronologically, NotaryNow notarizes a document. If each product solved "where do files live" on its own, the platform would end up with as many storage implementations, security models, and third-party integrations as it had products — instead of one.

That's why LitBox needed to exist before most of those products launched, not after. Shipping it first meant everything built afterward could assume a shared, owned file layer from day one, instead of retrofitting one in once four or five products already had their own quiet workarounds.

CasePro

  • Evidence and case files need to be tied to the matter record it manages — LitBox is where that evidence actually lives.

OmnisProof

  • E-signature needs a document to sign, in place, without a round trip through a downloaded file.

MedChron

  • Medical chronologies are built from records that need a single, permission-aware source instead of scattered copies.

NotaryNow

  • Notarization needs the same document trust model as e-signature — one owned layer, not a fourth vendor integration.

My Role

I led this end to end: framing the three problems above from patterns across the products I'd already worked on, the research that grounded the case for owning storage instead of integrating another vendor, the information architecture and permission model, the full UI system, and the integration points — like the "Import from LitBox" pattern used inside other Omnis AI products — that make LitBox a layer the rest of the ecosystem actually plugs into, not a fourth silo next to the other three.

03 / RESEARCH & SECURITY CONTEXT
Why It Matters

Why secure storage is a different problem for a law firm

A generic drive protects files. A law firm's drive has to protect privilege — client identities, litigation strategy, medical and financial records, and communications that are confidential by professional obligation, not just by preference. That distinction is what "secure storage" has to mean for LitBox, and it's worth grounding in real incidents rather than abstract risk language.

No single method could carry the case for owning a storage layer on its own — secondary research proves the risk is real, competitive teardown proves the gap is real, and internal conversations proved the demand across products was real. Why I'm Not Using These as Marketing All five incidents targeted large, well-resourced firms with existing security programs — none of them prove LitBox would have prevented anything, and I'm not implying otherwise. What they do establish: social engineering, not exotic malware, was the entry point in at least three of the five, and every firm's own IT/security staff were the impersonation target. That's a specific, citable pattern — "harden the human workflow around file requests," not "buy more encryption."

The Rule Lawyers Actually Have to Follow

Lawyers aren't just choosing security software out of caution — a real ethics rule requires it. ABA Model Rule 1.6(c) says a lawyer must take "reasonable efforts" to stop a client's information from leaking, on purpose or by accident. A companion rule says a lawyer has to understand the tools they're using well enough to know the risk. Neither rule names a specific product or certification to use — just "reasonable" and "informed." That's a lower bar than "encrypted" or "fully compliant," which is exactly why I'm not slapping either word on LitBox without proof behind it.

What "Secure" Actually Has to Include

Legal-software vendors (Clio, Sync) and incident write-ups from CloudLex converge on the same short list. This is describing their own practices, not independent research, but the list itself is a useful checklist against a consumer drive:

  • Encryption — while a file moves, and while it sits in storage.
  • Multi-factor login — a password alone isn't enough to get in.
  • Per-person, per-file roles — not one link anyone can forward.
  • A record of who did what — not just that a file was sent somewhere.

Sync's own material is blunt about the gap: consumer tools are "designed for a broad audience" and usually skip the access controls and audit trails a firm specifically needs.

What LitBox Does Not Claim LitBox does not currently claim SOC 2 certification, HIPAA compliance, zero-knowledge or end-to-end encryption, or attorney-client-privilege protection as shipped, verified capabilities. Encryption in transit and at rest, role-based permissions, and broader audit-export tooling are design targets reflected in the product's own module-architecture research — treat anything above not tied to a screenshot elsewhere in this case study as a proposed capability requiring technical and business validation, not a shipped guarantee. The two security-adjacent surfaces actually built and shown in this case study are per-file role sharing and the Audit Timeline, both in Section 10.
04 / COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
Why Not Just Use Drive or Dropbox

Established storage products aren't wrong — they're not built for a legal matter

Google Drive, Dropbox, and Box are mature, well-engineered products used by millions of people, including plenty of law firms. The honest question isn't "are they secure" — it's "do they model the thing a law firm actually organizes around: a matter, not a folder."

Google Drive / Dropbox General Purpose

  • Reliable, familiar, deep integration with everyday office tools.
  • Sharing is file- or folder-scoped, not matter-scoped — a link can outlive the reason it was created.
  • No native concept of a "case," a retention policy, or a legal hold.
  • Search finds a file by name or content, not by which matter it belongs to.

Box Enterprise Governance

  • Strongest of the general-purpose tools on admin controls, retention, and compliance reporting.
  • Still organizes around folders and enterprise groups, not legal matters or case teams.
  • No native link into document processing, e-signature, or case-management workflows built for law firms.

Sync.com Privacy-First

  • Markets end-to-end/zero-knowledge encryption as a core differentiator for sensitive files.
  • Still a standalone drive — no matter context, no case-team permission model, no ecosystem it plugs into.

LitBox Legal-First

  • Files and folders behave the way Drive/Dropbox users already expect — same learning curve, familiar patterns.
  • Adds a matter-aware permission model, an audit timeline, and File Requests built for external submitters.
  • Connects natively into AutoDoc and OmnisProof today, with CasePro and the rest of the ecosystem as the roadmap.

Competitive SWOT

The same three competitors, scored honestly against LitBox rather than dismissed outright — including where LitBox itself is genuinely weaker today.

Dropbox / Google Drive

  • Strengths — familiar to nearly every user, deep Office/Workspace integration, mature mobile and desktop sync.
  • Weaknesses — no matter context, folder- or file-scoped sharing only, no legal-specific audit trail.
  • Opportunities — a firm frustrated by re-uploading the same file into three tools is a switching moment.
  • Threats — both could add "legal mode" features faster than a startup can build ecosystem integrations.

Box

  • Strengths — the strongest admin/governance/retention tooling of the three general-purpose tools.
  • Weaknesses — organized around departments and folders, not matters; no native e-signature or document-intelligence handoff.
  • Opportunities — its own governance strength is proof firms will pay for compliance features — LitBox's Audit Timeline targets the same instinct.
  • Threats — Box already sells into the enterprise-legal segment LitBox needs; incumbency is a real barrier.

Sync.com

  • Strengths — zero-knowledge encryption marketed directly at privacy-conscious buyers, including law firms.
  • Weaknesses — still a standalone drive — no matter model, no case-team permissions, nothing to plug into.
  • Opportunities — proves firms will pay a premium for "more secure than Dropbox" positioning, which LitBox can pair with ecosystem integration.
  • Threats — the closest thing to a shortcut — a firm could get "secure enough" from Sync without ever needing LitBox, which is exactly the case made in Section 01's third problem.
Where LitBox Is Still Behind Drive, Dropbox, and Box have years of reliability data, mobile and desktop sync clients, and integrations LitBox doesn't have yet — Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and a mature third-party app ecosystem among them. A firm already deep in one of those tools has real switching costs LitBox has to justify, not assume away.
05 / SOLUTIONS CONSIDERED
Options on the Table

Three places the shared file layer could have lived. Two were faster to ship — and wrong

Once "every product needs files and none of them own storage" was clear (Section 02), the fix wasn't obvious yet — it rarely is. These are the three real architectural paths I weighed, not a polished single recommendation dressed up after the fact. The fuller research behind each option — module notes, competitor teardown, stakeholder input — lives as its own set of pages inside the project's Figma file, not just summarized here.

A — Each Product Keeps Its Own Storage

  • LitBox becomes a thin UI opened from inside AutoDoc or CasePro — no shared backend, just a nicer picker.
  • Fastest to ship — every product already has some upload mechanism to build on.

Rejected — doesn't fix fragmentation, it just re-skins it. Every product still holds its own copy of a file, with no shared audit trail connecting them.

B — Fold Storage Into CasePro

  • Build file management as a module inside the case-management CRM, since matters already live there.
  • The "obvious" answer — CasePro already owns the concept of a matter.

Rejected — ties every other product's file access to CasePro's release cycle and permission model. MedChron and LitDraft would still need their own workarounds to reach a file.

C — A Standalone Layer, Embeddable Everywhere

  • LitBox owns a file once, and exposes a search/picker surface any product can call into — not just link out to.
  • Slower to ship than A, more independent than B.

Chosen — the only option where AutoDoc, OmnisProof, MedChron, and LitDraft can each connect on their own timeline, without depending on each other or on CasePro shipping first.

The Part I Didn't Expect to Prove Out Option C was a bet that other product teams would actually build against LitBox rather than route around it. Section 15's real staging screenshots — LitBox's search running live inside MedChron and AutoDoc — are the closest thing this case study has to that bet paying off.
06 / PRODUCT STRATEGY
Defining the Product

Turning research into a defensible scope, not a feature wishlist

Problem Statement

Law firms working inside the Omnis AI ecosystem have no shared, secure place to store case files — so every product either builds its own storage or depends on a general-purpose drive that has no concept of a legal matter, a case team, or an audit trail.

Opportunity Statement

A file layer purpose-built for legal matters — with familiar Drive-like interaction patterns — can become the shared foundation the rest of the ecosystem reads from and writes to, instead of each product solving storage on its own.

Product Principles

Familiar First, Legal Second

The core file-management surface should feel like Drive or Dropbox on first use — the legal-specific value should layer on top, not force a new mental model for basic file handling.

Every Action Leaves a Record

Upload, download, share, permission change, restore, delete — the Audit Timeline exists so nothing sensitive happens invisibly.

A Platform, Not a Silo

A file uploaded to LitBox should be reachable from the products that need it — starting with AutoDoc and OmnisProof — instead of requiring a re-upload for every downstream tool.

Who It's For

Not every persona needs the same surface — that shaped the IA more than any single feature request did.

  • Attorneys — need fast retrieval and confidence that a shared file is the current version.
  • Paralegals / litigation support — the heaviest daily users: uploading, organizing, and requesting files from clients and co-counsel.
  • Firm admins / IT — own storage oversight, user provisioning, and audit exports; a distinct admin-facing surface (Storage Management, Audit Logs & Activity) exists specifically for this group.

MVP Scope

The internal-beta scope covers the core file-management loop end to end — upload, organize, preview, share, request, restore/delete, search, and audit — plus the confirmed ecosystem connections: AutoDoc via File Requests, OmnisProof via in-document e-signature, and embedded search live inside MedChron and AutoDoc itself (Section 15). A conversational AI assistant, deeper CasePro matter-linking, and broader third-party integrations are explicitly out of MVP scope; see Section 13 for what that AI question actually looks like.

07 / THE TRANSFORMATION
Before vs. After

A file's trip through four tools, or through one

The scenario below is an illustrative, research-informed composite — not a transcript of an observed user. I'm marking it that way deliberately, because the difference between "this is what I watched happen" and "this is a representative pattern the research supports" matters.

Before Assumption

  • Client emails a PDF; a paralegal downloads it and saves it into a personal or team Drive folder.
  • The same file gets re-uploaded into AutoDoc to run document processing.
  • A signed version is needed, so it's emailed out again for an e-signature tool.
  • Opposing counsel requests the file — it's shared via a public link with no expiry and no record of who opened it.
  • Six months later, nobody can say with confidence which copy was the final one.

After LitBox

  • File is uploaded once into LitBox and organized into a folder tied to the matter.
  • AutoDoc reads the file directly through the File Requests → AutoDoc Documents pipeline — no re-upload.
  • E-signature is launched in place from the document's own detail view, no export/re-import step.
  • External submissions come back through a File Request with an expiry date, an optional password, and a defined destination folder.
  • Every upload, share, permission change, and deletion is written to the Audit Timeline.
The value of LitBox isn't that any one of these steps is impossible today — it's that today, each step is a separate decision, in a separate tool, with no shared record connecting them.

Two Core Flows Worth Walking Through

Uploading into a File Request, end to end. A paralegal opens File Requests → Create New Request, sets a title, a destination folder, an optional deadline and password, and shares it with an external email. 09 / THE WORKSPACE
Dashboard & File Management

Designing for the file, and for the moment someone can't access it

Most of a file-management product's design work isn't the happy path — it's what a grid looks like with zero files in it, what happens when someone opens a folder they don't have permission for, and what a delete confirmation says when the action can't be undone after a set window. LitBox's Figma file has more than two dozen empty, error, and permission states designed up front, across core file management, sharing, search, audit, and admin — that scope shaped the workspace as much as the dashboard did.

Three Small Decisions, Kept Small On Screen

Upload favors clarity over cleverness. Restore stays quiet — reversible, one click. Delete inside the retention window costs a second's friction on purpose, so nothing sensitive disappears silently.

A Named Person, Not a Link Anyone Can Forward

Section 03 named the problem: a public link has no idea who still has it. LitBox's answer is that sharing a file means picking named people and giving each one a specific role — Owner, Editor, or Viewer — instead of generating a link. There's no "anyone with the link can view" option to reach for.

The Audit Timeline — the One Security Surface Actually Built

Every upload, download, share, rename, permission change, and restore is written to a single timeline, filterable by action type, with the file, the user and role, and a timestamp on every row. Alongside the roles above, this is the concrete answer to "how would a firm know who touched a file" — not a proposed capability, something actually built.

Admin Gets a Different Dashboard, Not a Hidden Toggle

Storage Management is a separate surface for firm admins — total usage, storage by file type, per-user breakdowns, and quota alerts — rather than a setting buried inside the same view every attorney and paralegal sees. Keeping it separate meant the day-to-day file workspace never had to compromise its simplicity to accommodate governance features most users will never open.

Real Iteration, Not Just a First Draft A dedicated UI-testing pass on these screens produced direct, unglamorous feedback I then designed against — things like tightening card padding, applying minimum card widths so a grid doesn't collapse awkwardly on narrower viewports, adding tooltips for truncated file and folder names instead of letting them clip silently, and standardizing sidebar and header components against the shared component library rather than letting each screen drift. None of that shows up in a polished screenshot, but it's the difference between a mockup and a system someone else can extend.

The Component Language Behind Every Screen

LitBox doesn't run its own isolated visual language — it's built on the same shared Omnis AI design system every other product in the ecosystem draws from, with LitBox-specific components (the Storage Widget, the Audit Timeline row, File Request cards) layered on top of the shared tokens and primitives. Every screen in this case study draws from that same small set of tokens and components, not a one-off style per page. 11 / FIVE FEATURES, IN DETAIL
What Ships in the Internal Beta

Five surfaces that carry most of the daily weight

Everything below is a real screen from the shipped Figma file, not a mockup built for this case study.

1. Dashboard, With Full Document Detail

The Dashboard is deliberately data-dense on purpose — storage usage, file/folder counts, and recent activity at a glance, so a firm admin isn't hunting for that information in a separate settings screen. Opening any file surfaces a detail drawer with three views stitched into two tabs: Document Details (who has access, folder metadata, description) and Document Activity (a timeline of edits, renames, and inline comments from the team).

2. Sharing, With Per-Person Roles

Rather than one public link everyone gets the same access to, sharing a file assigns each person a role — Owner, Editor, or Viewer — by email, the way the rest of this case study's honesty framework requires: no overclaiming a password/expiry system that isn't in the shipped file yet. The reference alongside it is what a fuller privacy-and-password layer looks like in a mature product, and the direction the sharing model is headed.

3. Storage Management, Admin Only

A separate permission tier, not a hidden toggle in the same screen everyone sees. Firm admins get organization-wide storage totals, a breakdown by file type, and a per-user table with quota usage — the same screen that would back the "Export Report" a firm's IT lead needs for a compliance review.

4. Tag Management, Finder-Style

Colored tags that work across file types — a document, an image, and a video can all carry the same tag — closer to how macOS Finder tags work than to a folder-only organization scheme. A file can carry more than one tag, so "matter" and "document type" don't have to compete for the same folder slot.

5. Global Search

Search scoped to All Modules, Files/Documents, Folders, or Recent Files, with a clear empty state rather than a blank screen when a query comes back with nothing. Section 12 covers what a populated result actually looks like — path, page, and highlighted snippet — once AutoDoc has extracted a file's text.

Search, and the AI Question

Search that reads what AutoDoc already extracted — and an AI assistant that isn't built yet

Two different things live in this section, and I want to keep them clearly separate: content search across AutoDoc-processed documents is designed and functioning in the prototype today. A conversational AI assistant is a direction worth exploring — and explicitly not something LitBox has built.

How Global Search Actually Works

Why This Matters More Than It Looks

"Search inside documents" sounds like a checkbox feature until you consider what it actually required: LitBox's search only works this way because AutoDoc had already extracted text from the file. Search got meaningfully better specifically because LitBox and AutoDoc share a file — not because of anything search-specific LitBox built on its own. Section 15 shows what that sharing looks like from the other direction.

The AI Assistant Question

The module-architecture research behind LitBox sketches use cases like "find all medical records related to this matter" or "summarize these documents" — a conversational layer on top of content search. That thinking has since moved from research notes into concept screens, covered next in Section 13. None of it is shipped or built into the product today — any answer would need a source citation back to the exact file and page it came from, retrieval would need to respect the same permission model as manual search, and it would sit on top of the existing search index rather than replace it.

Everything in this section is a concept exploration, not a shipped feature — I want that stated plainly before anything else. They're the current best answer to a real design question: once content search and permissions already work (Sections 12 and 10), what does a conversational layer on top of them actually need to look like?

The module-architecture research (Section 03) sketched twelve possible AI workflows. Five are worth showing here — the ones that build most directly on what's already real: content search and the permission model.

1. Natural-Language File Search

  • "Find the documents related to the Smith accident that mention vehicle damage" instead of keyword search — returns files with a stated reason each one matched.

2. Document Summary & Q&A

  • Ask a question about a file's contents and get an answer traceable back to the exact page — the same citation requirement manual search already meets.

3. Multi-File Comparison & Extraction

  • Compare two contract versions clause by clause, with additions, removals, and modifications called out per section.

4. Image & Video Understanding

  • Search and reason over evidence photos and video, not just text documents — a gap none of LitBox's current search covers.

5. AI-Assisted Organization & Actions

  • Find related files across a matter, flag missing documents, and take a scoped action — always inside the same per-person permission boundary manual sharing already enforces.
Proposed Capability — Requires Technical and Business Validation A conversational AI assistant, hallucination handling, and any claim about the accuracy or confidentiality of AI-generated answers are unbuilt, unvalidated concepts. "AI can make mistakes — always verify important information" is written directly into the concept screens themselves, not added as a footnote here.

16 / SECURITY & TRUST
Why This Matters Specifically for a Law Firm

Every pattern in this section exists because privilege, not preference, is on the line

Section 03 made the case with documented breaches and an ethics rule; this section is the shorter, practical version — the six trust surfaces that actually show up in the product, gathered in one place instead of scattered across the case study.

Permission-Based Access

  • Every file's visibility is scoped to named people and their role, not "anyone with the org login" — the same per-person model shown in Section 11's sharing feature.

Secure File Sharing

  • Owner, Editor, or Viewer, assigned per person, per file — no default public-link option to reach for when a scoped share would take one extra click.

Clear Access Visibility

  • The Document Details drawer's "Who has access" list (Section 11) means a paralegal can check who can see a file without opening a separate admin screen.

Audit Logs & Activity

  • Upload, download, share, permission change, restore, delete — every action lands in the Audit Timeline, the one surface Product Principles (Section 06) singles out by name.

Secure File Requests

  • External submitters never get a LitBox account — a request carries a deadline, an optional password, and a defined destination folder instead of an open-ended inbox.

AI Permission Awareness

  • Section 13's concept screens mark every AI result "You have access" or "Restricted" before anything else — a design requirement, not a feature the assistant gets to skip.
Restating What's Actually Shipped Permission-based sharing, File Requests with expiry/password, and the Audit Timeline are built and shown in this case study with real screens. Encryption at rest, SOC 2, and HIPAA compliance are not shipped claims — Section 03 already says so, and I'm not going to say something different here just because this section reads more like a trust pitch.
17 / PRODUCT VALUE
Why Would a Firm Switch

A balanced case, not a "we're just better" claim

"Lawyers will switch because LitBox is more secure" isn't a defensible claim — Drive, Dropbox, and Box are all reasonably secure products. The actual case for LitBox has to rest on something more specific.

What Would Motivate a Switch

  • Already using AutoDoc or OmnisProof and tired of re-uploading the same file into each one.
  • Need an audit trail for file activity that a general drive doesn't natively provide.
  • Want File Requests with expiry and password controls instead of an open-ended public link.
  • Consolidating tools rather than adding one more login to an already-fragmented stack.

What Would Stop a Switch

  • Migration effort — years of files already organized in an existing drive.
  • No SOC 2, HIPAA, or other third-party certification to point to yet.
  • Missing desktop sync, mobile apps, and the Microsoft 365 / Google Workspace integrations firms already depend on.
  • Trust in an early-stage internal-beta product versus an incumbent with a decade of track record.

The defensible pitch isn't "more secure" — it's "the file layer that gets more useful the more of Omnis AI you already use." That's a narrower claim, and it's one the File Requests → AutoDoc and in-document e-signature flows can actually back up today.

18 / FINAL EXPERIENCE & IMPACT
Where This Stands

What I can claim, what I can't yet, and what I'd do differently

What Actually Changed for the Ecosystem

Zoom out from any single screen and the shift is structural: Omnis AI went from a set of products that each quietly solved "where do files live" on its own, to a platform with one file layer the rest of it reads from and writes to. AutoDoc picks up files without a manual re-upload. OmnisProof signs a document in place. MedChron and LitDraft search the same index LitBox itself searches. None of that required a firm to change how they work — it required Omnis AI to stop asking every product to solve storage twice.

For the Firm

  • One place to upload a file instead of one per tool it needs to reach.
  • A permission model and audit trail built for a legal matter, not adapted from a generic drive.
  • Fewer uncontrolled public links, because a scoped File Request or a per-person role is the default path, not an extra step.

For the Ecosystem

  • New products — NotaryNow, deeper CasePro matter-linking — can assume a shared file layer exists instead of building their own.
  • Search and AI-assisted workflows (Section 13) get more useful the more products connect to the same index, not less.
  • A file's security posture no longer depends on which third-party vendor happened to hold it that week.

None of this makes LitBox "done." It makes the third problem from Section 01 — an ecosystem that wasn't actually one — no longer true by default. That's the specific claim this case study is willing to make.

Impact — Honestly Framed

Measured

None yet. LitBox is in internal beta with no usage baseline to report — I'm not going to invent adoption numbers or time-savings percentages to make this section look stronger.

Observed

AutoDoc and OmnisProof integrations work end to end in the prototype, and staging screenshots confirm search is already live inside MedChron and AutoDoc. A structured UI-QA pass produced concrete, actionable fixes I designed against — real iteration, even without formal usability-test data.

Expected

Reduced re-uploading across AutoDoc/OmnisProof workflows, a defensible audit trail for firms handling sensitive files, and fewer uncontrolled public links — all hypotheses to validate once real usage exists, not claims to make today.

My Role

I owned LitBox's product design end to end: the competitive and module-architecture research that scoped what "parity with Dropbox and Box" actually required, the information architecture, the full UI system across file management, sharing, search, audit, and admin, and the design of its live ecosystem connections into AutoDoc, OmnisProof, MedChron, and LitDraft. The empty-state, error-state, and permission-state documentation — more than two dozen scenarios specified before a single one shipped — was mine to define, not a gap engineering had to fill in later.

What I Learned

Shared infrastructure is a different job

  • LitBox's users aren't just attorneys and paralegals — they're also AutoDoc and OmnisProof. A broken assumption in a file's metadata breaks someone else's product, not just this one.

A parity checklist isn't a strategy

  • The Dropbox/Box feature-parity research was necessary groundwork, but the real differentiation came from two questions a parity table can't answer: which matter does this belong to, and who downstream needs it.

Security language has to earn its claims

  • It was easier to write "SOC 2 compliant" into an early feature list than to confirm it was true. Separating planned from verified capability was a discipline I had to actively maintain, not a default.

Edge cases first is what makes it feel real

  • Designing 25+ empty and error states before the happy path ships is the difference between a prototype and a demo that only works when everything goes right.
LitBox's real test isn't whether its file grid looks as good as Dropbox's. It's whether AutoDoc and OmnisProof keep needing fewer workarounds the more of it ships — that's the metric an infrastructure product actually has to answer to.
