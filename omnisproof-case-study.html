All work Case study Legal tech AI

From manual document setup to effortless signing.

Legal teams shouldn't have to wrestle with complicated workflows to send a document for signature. The task is straightforward, yet it often involves multiple steps, hidden documents, and repetitive field placement. All of that adds friction before a document reaches the signer.

I redesigned OmnisProof's signing setup so legal teams move from document to signature with less effort: upload, field placement and AI assistance in one self-serve flow.

The problem

Sending a document for signature took a 4-step modal wizard, hidden documents and repetitive field placement before it reached the signer.

What I did

Redesigned setup as one guided page where the document stays in view, and added reviewable AI field detection: the AI proposes fields, the user approves them.

Where it stands

Shipped. Contract execution went from 11 steps to 8, and about 72% of AI field suggestions were accepted without edits in 6 internal prototype walkthroughs.

01What we're building

Making document signing simpler from the very first step.

OmnisProof is a dedicated e-signing platform built for high-velocity legal workflows. We eliminated disjointed modal wizards and automated manual field placement into a single guided workspace. The redesign tackles three major bottlenecks across the preparation lifecycle.

01

Too many steps before sending a document

The problem

Preparing a document for signature involved moving through multiple screens and disconnected steps. Users had to work through the setup process before they could place signature fields.

What we're building

A single, guided workspace where users can upload a document, configure signing details, and prepare it for sending, all in one continuous flow.

02

Manual field placement slows everything down

The problem

Placing signature fields manually across lengthy legal documents is repetitive, time-consuming, and easy to get wrong. Users had to scan documents to determine where each person needed to sign.

What we're building

AI-assisted field detection that identifies potential signing locations and suggests where fields belong, while keeping users in control of reviewing and confirming them.

03

Making document preparation easier to manage

The problem

Legal teams work with different document types, signing requirements, and workflows. The experience needed to support these variations without adding more complexity.

What we're building

A flexible signing experience that supports document uploads, reusable templates, and existing files, giving teams more ways to start and complete their signing workflow.

The goal

Turn document signing from a multi-step chore into a straightforward, guided experience that legal teams can complete with confidence.

02The product

The document execution layer inside a legal AI ecosystem.

OmnisProof is the document execution layer of the Omnis legal AI ecosystem. It turns a drafted agreement into a signed, filed legal record: prepare, assign signers, place fields, send and track, without leaving the platform.

It sounds like the easy part. But drafting, matter tracking and client portals all produce documents that end up here. If signing is slow or error-prone, it undermines everything upstream.

Situation before this project

Engineering had shipped a working MVP: a 4-step wizard (General Info → Add Signers → Add Fields → Distribute) modelled on e-signature admin panels. It covered every setting, but not how lawyers work. The document didn't appear until step three.

Why this became urgent

Leadership pitched OmnisProof as a full DocuSign replacement. That pitch fails if our setup is slower and clumsier than DocuSign's. When I took over, that gap was the most-cited risk in product reviews.

03Why we solved this

We didn't have time for a full research cycle. We looked at the strongest signals available.

No research budget and no access to law firms before launch. So I combined all the signals that existed: walkthroughs with 2 lawyers and a paralegal, the PM's testing notes, a teardown of 5 competitors, and G2 & Capterra reviews.

The same pattern kept showing up across all sources:

  • Users could complete each step of the wizard but lost track of which document they were configuring
  • Manual field placement was the single largest source of both time spent and errors made
  • Reviewers of competitor tools described documents sent with a missing field or the wrong recipient, discovered only after the fact

Walkthroughs surfaced it, the PM's testing reproduced it, and reviews showed a category-wide failure mode nobody had solved. That's why it beat other roadmap items.

04Research

Configuring a document correctly was the hard part. Getting it into the system was easy.

I ran a structured teardown of DocuSign, Adobe Sign, Dropbox Sign, PandaDoc, and Documenso: their public documentation, review-site screenshots, and (where available) free-tier trials.

What competitors do well
Where they fall short
Document upload and recipient invitation are fast and well-documented across all five platforms.
Field placement is manual: click, drag, resize, repeat, for every signature block and date field on every page.
DocuSign offers limited auto-placement for a narrow set of pre-formatted templates.
Outside that narrow case, nothing in the category offers reviewable AI assistance for field detection.
Send flows are optimized to be fast: most competitors market “send in under a minute.”
That speed costs verification. G2 reviews describe documents sent with a missing field or wrong recipient, caught only after the fact.
Pattern across all five: getting a document in is solved; making it correct once it's there is not.

Key insight: getting a document in is solved everywhere, including our MVP. The unsolved part sits between "uploaded" and "ready to send": field placement, recipients, and catching mistakes before they go out.

That reframed the direction of the whole project: let the system handle more of the setup, and let users review and correct only what needs their attention, instead of making manual configuration slightly faster.

05Constraints & decisions

Not "redesign the interface": decide which complexity stays with the user, and which the system should absorb.

Constraint 01

Field placement was required, but too heavy to leave manual

Why this mattered
Every document needs signature, date, name, and initials fields placed correctly before it can go out. This step couldn't be removed, but making users place every field on every page by hand was the largest time cost we'd identified.
Decision
Turn field placement from a manual task into a system-assisted one: AI detects and proposes placement, the user reviews and corrects only where needed. Deep dive 01 →
Constraint 02

The 4-step structure mapped to something real, but hiding the document behind it didn't

Why this mattered
"Who's involved → what goes where → is it ready to send" is how document prep works. The three-stage logic was fine. Presenting each stage as a separate full screen hid the document being configured.
Decision
Keep the underlying stages, rebuild the presentation as one page: the document stays visible at all times, with a persistent step tracker and a side panel that changes per stage. Deep dive 02 →
Constraint 03

No direct access to external users

Why this mattered
Each decision below needed some form of validation, but law firms weren't reachable pre-launch. Relying only on internal opinion risked designing for people who already trust the product.
Decision
Validate with internal legal professionals as a proxy, cross-check against competitive review-site complaints as external signal, and label each claim with its actual confidence level instead of letting it read as more validated than it is.
Constraint 04

The fix had to ship inside the existing timeline

Why this mattered
The competitive gap was already being cited as a risk in product reviews. A ground-up redesign of the entire setup experience wasn't realistic in the time available.
Decision
Scope to the two moments with the clearest evidence of friction (field placement and the wizard structure) rather than trying to fix every part of document prep at once.

06Deep dive 01 · AI-assisted field placement

How do you make legal professionals trust an AI that places their signature fields?

Placing every field by hand, for every recipient, took the largest share of prep time and caused each placement mistake we saw. Automating was easy. Trust was hard: a wrong AI guess sends a contract to the wrong person.

Testing three ways to introduce AI field detection

Iteration 1 · rejected

Automatic, silent placement

AI detects and places each field the moment a document is uploaded. No review step: the fields are there, ready to send.

Why it failed: a lawyer on our team moved each AI-placed field, even the correct ones: "I didn't see it place them, so I don't trust them." The checking work just moved somewhere with no support.

Iteration 2 · rejected

Chat-based AI assistant

A conversational interface where the user could type "place a signature field for Lucas on page 3" and the AI would execute it.

Why it was rejected: field placement is one-time setup, not a conversation. Typing instructions added a step and a new pattern to learn, for a task people want done in seconds.

Final · shipped

Inline, reviewable "Detect with AI"

One button inside the existing field panel. Clicking it places suggested fields on the document, using the same drag-and-edit interaction as a manually placed field.

Why it worked: the placement is visible as it happens, each suggestion is editable or deletable with no new interaction to learn, and there's no separate "accept" step. The AI proposes and the user decides, using tools they already know.

Designing for failure and non-standard inputs

Edge case

Non-standard or custom field types that fall outside the seven types the model recognizes (signature, date, name, initials, title, email, text) aren't guessed at: they're left for manual placement using the same field library, with no error state implying something went wrong.

Edge case

Scanned documents with non-selectable text can't be reliably parsed by the detection model. "Detect with AI" stays visible but shows a clear inline message explaining why detection isn't available for this file, instead of failing or returning wrong guesses.

Edge case

A user rejects most suggestions on one document. The next document still gets a fresh detection pass. The real risk is someone deciding "the AI doesn't work" and switching it off. Trust recovery is untested, so I flag it as open.

Exploring layouts for progressive disclosure

Iteration 1 · rejected

Delete the steps

Strip the wizard down to one dense screen: recipients, fields, document preview, and settings all visible at once. Fewer screens, assumed to mean faster.

Why it failed: a lawyer called it "a cockpit", and neither tester knew where to start. Removing screens doesn't remove complexity; it squeezes it onto one screen.

Iteration 2 · rejected

Merge General Info into Add Signers

A smaller fix: combine the first two steps into one, going from four screens to three, keeping the rest of the wizard structure intact.

Why it was rejected: it reduced the click count but didn't touch the actual complaint: the document was still hidden until step two of three. A marginal improvement to the wrong metric.

Final · shipped

Same 3-stage logic, one persistent page

Keep Document & Recipients → Add Fields → Review & Send as the underlying structure, but render it as three states of a single page: a persistent step tracker on the left, the document centered, and a right-hand panel whose content changes per stage.

Why it worked: the document never disappears behind its own configuration. The underlying logic stayed the same; only how much of it stayed visible changed.

Handling changes and missing fields

Edge case

A recipient is added after fields are already placed. Any field already assigned to a now-invalid configuration is flagged inline in the field panel instead of being reassigned, so the user makes the call on where it belongs.

Edge case

A required field is missing at Review & Send. Step 2 gets flagged and Send stays disabled with an inline reason: the same error pattern as field mapping, so users learn one style.

What survived from the original wizard, on purposeThe three-stage logic itself. Deleting steps to "reduce friction" is a common trap: the wizard's sequence (who's involved → what goes where → is it ready) was correct information architecture. The failure was in the presentation, not the sequence, so I kept the sequence and rebuilt the presentation.

The same two lawyers who were lost in both the old wizard and the "cockpit" completed the new flow without a single question. Small sample, but the same people and task, before and after.

08Outcome

The complexity didn't disappear. It moved from the user to the system.

The two heaviest jobs moved to the system: AI proposes fields, and the three stages live on one page where the document never leaves view. Users now review and correct instead of configuring blind.

09Final thoughts

Designing for confidence as well as completion.

Redesigning OmnisProof taught me to simplify a workflow by making each step clear, purposeful, and easy to recover from.

Legal teams need to send documents quickly and need confidence that the right document reaches the right people, with the right fields, without extra back-and-forth.

01

Put the document at the center

Users should keep sight of the thing they're working on. Keeping the document visible turned a configuration-heavy process into an understandable workspace.

02

Let AI handle the repetitive work

AI is most useful when it removes tedious tasks while keeping users in control. Suggested fields reduce manual effort, but review and approval remain with the person preparing the document.

03

Clear structure over false simplicity

The signing workflow involved documents, recipients, fields, permissions, and delivery settings. I organized that complexity so users could move through it.

What I'd explore next

With more time and access to real usage data, I'd focus on:

  • Testing the redesigned workflow with external law firms.
  • Understanding how users interact with AI suggestions over time.
  • Exploring smarter detection for complex document fields.
  • Measuring setup time, completion rates, and correction patterns.
Closing thought

Great product design doesn't remove complexity. It makes complexity feel effortless.

OmnisProof gave me a chance to rethink how legal teams prepare documents: keep the essential steps and make each interaction clearer, more intentional, and easier to trust.

Less manual work, more clarity, and more confidence in each signature. That's the kind of experience I believe legal technology should deliver.

10Questions I expect

Anticipated questions on these two problems.

Why only two problems instead of the whole product?
Going deep on two hard problems shows how I think better than a product tour. Both had a clear arc: a rejected first attempt, evidence of why, and a validated fix.
How confident are you in the 72% AI-acceptance number?
Moderately. It's from six internal walkthroughs with legal professionals in our own organization, not a controlled study or production telemetry. I treat it as directional evidence the detection is useful, not a validated product metric, and I say that up front.
Why didn't you just delete the wizard's steps to simplify it?
I tried it first: one dense screen with everything visible. A tester called it "a cockpit." The three stages were the right structure; hiding the document behind them was the mistake.
What would you do differently with more time or budget?
Test with external law firm users instead of internal proxies, and instrument real production metrics instead of relying on six walkthrough sessions. That gap, between what I believe and what I can prove at scale, is my answer.
